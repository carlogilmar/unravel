## The PR you couldn't review

That feeling — *"I opened the PR and didn't know what to review; the code looks better than mine"* — is the single most important signal in this whole document. It is worth sitting with rather than rushing past, because it contains a category error that, once named, dissolves a lot of the anxiety.

You were trying to review the **code**. But the thing that actually needs a human is not the code. It is the **theory** the code is supposed to embody — and the AI never had one.

The rest of this post is an attempt to make that claim precise, to ground it in 40 years of writing about what software development actually *is*, and to turn it into a concrete answer to your two questions: *what is my role now, and where are the opportunities?*

---

## What "collaborative software" actually means

The phrase has two senses, and the AI moment fuses them.

**Sense 1 — software *is* collaboration.** Long before LLMs, building software was never a solitary act of typing. It was people coordinating: pair programming (one *driver* typing, one *navigator* reasoning aloud and reviewing every line — Kent Beck's XP), code review, design discussions, the shared mental model a team carries. Donald Knuth's literate programming and the SICP maxim — *"programs must be written for people to read, and only incidentally for machines to execute"* (Abelson & Sussman) — both say the same thing: **code is a communication medium between humans first, and an instruction to a machine second.** Gerald Weinberg's *The Psychology of Computer Programming* (1971) made the social dimension explicit decades ago with "egoless programming" — the discipline of separating your identity from your code so that a better approach than yours is a *gift*, not a threat. (Hold onto that — it speaks directly to "the code looks better than mine.")

**Sense 2 — software is now collaboration *with a non-human*.** The AI is a new kind of teammate: fast, tireless, widely-read, and — critically — *without a self, a memory of your system, or accountability*. The research community calls this the **centaur** model, borrowed from Garry Kasparov's "advanced chess," where a human + engine beats either alone. The collaboration patterns people have identified map cleanly onto coding: a **guidance model** (you steer step by step), a **sketch model** (the AI roughs it in, you refine), and an **inverted-control model** (the AI drives, you supervise). Most real work flips between them hour to hour.

The fusion: software was *always* a collaborative, communicative act among people who shared a theory of the system. AI adds a participant that can produce the *artifact* (code, PR description, ticket) without ever sharing the *theory*. Your job is shifting toward being the keeper of that theory.

## The idea that resolves your anxiety: programming as theory building

In 1985 Peter Naur — of Backus–Naur form — wrote a short, now-famous essay, *Programming as Theory Building*. Its thesis:

> "Programming in essence is building a certain kind of theory, a theory of how certain affairs of the world will be handled by, or supported by, a computer program."

The radical part: **the program is not the source code.** The program is the *theory* living in the programmers' minds — why it is shaped this way, what the world it models is doing, where it can safely change, what would break and why. The source text is a lossy projection of that theory. Naur argued the theory **cannot be fully recovered from the code, the docs, or the comments** — which is why throwing a new team at an unfamiliar codebase rarely revives a dead project: the theory died even though the text survived.

Now apply that to your PR. An LLM produced clean, idiomatic text — possibly *better* text than you would have written, because it has read more code than any human ever will. But it **holds no theory**. It cannot tell you why this shape and not another, what the blast radius of a change is, which lines encode a hard-won domain constraint versus an arbitrary default. Worse, as Sean Goedecke and others have noted, an agent **rebuilds its theory from scratch on every invocation** — it is structurally incapable of accumulating the thing that makes a senior engineer valuable.

So "I didn't know what to review" has a precise answer: **you weren't missing a skill; you were missing the theory the code is supposed to encode — and so was the AI.** Review, in the AI era, is the act of *constructing that theory yourself* and checking the artifact against it. This is exactly why a junior who accepts code they don't understand creates what one writer calls "theoretically orphaned" code: text with no living theory behind it anywhere in the building.

> This is also the through-line to your other documents and to Unravel itself: the tool exists to help you *build the theory* of code you didn't write, because the AI cannot hand it to you.

## Why your role didn't disappear — it moved up the stack

Fred Brooks' *No Silver Bullet* (1986) drew the other essential distinction. Software difficulty splits into:

- **Accidental complexity** — the incidental friction of the medium: syntax, boilerplate, remembering an API signature, wiring glue code.
- **Essential complexity** — the irreducible difficulty of *understanding what to build and why*: conflicting requirements, domain modeling, trade-offs you live with for years.

Every tool advance — compilers, IDEs, Stack Overflow — has attacked *accidental* complexity. **LLMs are the largest assault on accidental complexity in history.** They make the typing nearly free. But Brooks' point, which has held for forty years and still holds, is that **no tool touches essential complexity** — because deciding what should exist, and owning the consequences, is not a typing problem.

That is the structural reason your role survives: AI ate the part of the job that was accidental (producing plausible text) and left untouched, even *amplified*, the part that was essential (deciding, judging, owning). The bottleneck moved from *how fast can you write it* to *how well can you decide what to build and verify that this is it*.

---

## The role, concretely: coder → conductor → orchestrator

Addy Osmani (Google) and Steve Yegge (Sourcegraph) independently describe the same trajectory:

- **Coder (pre-2024)** — you write the lines; AI is fancy autocomplete.
- **Conductor (2024–2025)** — you work closely with *one* agent on one task, steering it turn by turn, intervening, iterating in real time.
- **Orchestrator (2026)** — you coordinate an *ensemble* of agents, each with its own context, working asynchronously. You plan the work, assign it, verify it, and hold the whole together. Yegge runs 20–30 parallel agents and claims "the IDE is dead"; Osmani's O'Reilly piece frames the engineer as the conductor who provides "vision, judgment, and orchestration while AI handles implementation details."

Be skeptical of the hype around the edges (the IDE is not literally dead, and "20–30 agents" is not most people's Tuesday). But the *direction* is real and backed by data: the 2025 DORA report found 90% AI adoption, and surveys show ~65% of developers expect their role redefined toward architecture, integration, and AI-enabled decision-making. The market is bifurcating: roles defined by **judgment** (architecting, evaluating AI output for correctness/security, building AI-powered features) are growing; roles defined by **task execution** (writing well-specified functions) are under pressure.

## Your new job description — the skills that rose in value

If typing is cheap, what are you *for*? Concretely, the high-value work is now:

1. **Theory-holding.** Be the person who can explain *why* the system is shaped this way and what breaks if it changes. This is Naur's irreplaceable core. Unravel's per-hunk "why" notes are a tool for exactly this.
2. **Problem framing & specification.** The spec is now the bottleneck, not the code (the entire spec-driven-development movement is built on this). Deciding *what* to build, with crisp scope and constraints, is leverage that multiplies across every agent you run.
3. **Verification & judgment ("taste").** The 2026 hiring signal is explicit: prove you "ship real work *and* can tell when the AI is wrong." Taste — recognizing the subtly-wrong, the over-engineered, the insecure, the off-domain — is the differentiator. It is learnable and it compounds.
4. **Systems thinking & architecture.** Agents are locally plausible and globally inconsistent. Holding the cross-file, cross-service coherence is human work.
5. **Domain knowledge.** The AI knows code in general; it does not know *your* billing rules, *your* compliance constraints, *your* users. This is where generic-good code meets specifically-correct code.
6. **Accountability.** This is the one thing an AI structurally *cannot* provide, and Simon Willison is the sharpest voice on it: an agent "has neither accountability nor a reputation… it can't take responsibility for what it's done." Critical systems require *verifiable human accountability*. When you merge AI code, you are not approving text — you are **staking your name on a theory you have made yours.**

Notice these are not new skills invented for the AI age. They are the parts of senior engineering that were always the real job — now separated cleanly from the typing that used to disguise them.

## Opportunities

The optimistic read, and it's well-supported:

- **You can operate above your old throughput ceiling.** Greenfield and exploratory work see large speedups (METR found ~90% gains on greenfield even while measuring slowdowns on familiar code). Ideas that were "too much work to try" are now cheap to prototype.
- **Taste becomes a visible, marketable asset.** A blog post explaining a trade-off, a review where you pushed back on a bad AI pattern, a spec that an agent executed cleanly — these are now portfolio pieces. Judgment is hireable in a way that raw output no longer is.
- **The senior/theory-holder premium grows.** As code volume explodes, the scarce resource is people who can build and transfer the *theory* behind it. That is a durable, compounding career position.
- **AI as a tutor, not just a typist.** The developers who *gain* skill from AI (vs. atrophy) share one habit: they ask *why*, request explanations, and engage rather than delegate. You can deliberately use AI to accelerate your own theory-building.
- **You get to do more of the interesting part.** If you found the essential complexity (the deciding, the modeling, the trade-offs) more rewarding than the accidental (the boilerplate), the balance of your day is shifting toward the part you liked.

---

## The risks to take seriously

Opportunities have a shadow; name it so you can manage it:

- **Trust creep.** Willison's honest admission: as agents prove themselves, "review procedures can become looser." The moment you stop reading because it's *usually* fine is the moment a theory-orphaned bug ships. Make review a deliberate practice, not a vibe.
- **Skill atrophy / cognitive offloading.** Controlled studies (Anthropic, Microsoft/CMU) show measurable mastery drops when AI is used as a crutch. Use it as a collaborator you interrogate, not an oracle you obey.
- **The illusion of understanding.** "The code looks better than mine" can mean it *is* better — or that it's plausible enough to disarm your scrutiny. Egoless programming (Weinberg) cuts both ways: welcome the better approach, but don't let polish substitute for your theory of why it's correct.

## What to do Monday

A practical translation, especially for reviewing that PR:

1. **Read the spec/ticket before the diff.** Reconstruct *intent* first; you cannot judge fidelity against code alone.
2. **Form and write a one-line hypothesis** of what the change does, then read to confirm or be surprised. Surprises are the findings.
3. **Interrogate the "why," not the "what."** For each meaningful hunk: why this shape? what's the blast radius of changing it? which lines encode a real constraint? If you can't answer, you don't yet hold the theory — keep reading.
4. **Run it. Break it.** Empty, null, huge, malformed inputs. Plausible ≠ correct.
5. **Externalize the theory you build** — a note in your own words. That note is the durable artifact the AI can't produce. (This is the Unravel loop.)
6. **Sign with intent.** When you approve, you're taking accountability the AI cannot. Mean it.

## How this connects to Unravel

Everything above is the *why* behind the product:

- Unravel is a **theory-building tool** for code you didn't author. It refuses summaries on purpose — a summary would hand you text without theory, the exact trap.
- The **per-hunk "why" note** is Naur's theory, externalized and made yours.
- The **modification test** ("could I change this safely?") is the operational definition of *holding the theory*.
- The product's posture — human as the accountable theory-holder, AI as the prolific-but-theory-less author — is precisely the role this document argues you should step into.

## One-line summary

> The AI writes the text; you hold the theory. Your job didn't vanish when typing became free — it concentrated into the parts that were always the real work: deciding what to build, judging whether it's right, understanding why it's shaped this way, and being the one accountable when it ships. That is what "collaborative software" means now: a prolific author with no theory and no accountability, paired with a human who supplies both.
